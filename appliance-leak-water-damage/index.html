Appliance Leak Water Damage in Ocala: What to Know

Nearly every appliance leak we handle comes from one of three sources. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. With a dishwasher the leak is typically slow, coming from the door seal or the drain hose where the cabinets hide it. Behind a refrigerator, the thin tube feeding the ice maker may drip for months. Cleanup is different for each of them.

Our Ocala crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. Laundry room water goes into the wall at the back of the machine and the neighboring rooms, and with an upstairs laundry, the ceiling underneath. Standing water is extracted, absorbed moisture is measured, and drying equipment is sized to fit the wet area.

Before We Arrive

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceStop the cycle and unplug the machine if the outlet and floor around it are dry. If not, switch it off at the breaker instead.
  2. Close the Supply ValveWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Mop Up What You CanGetting reachable water up with a mop or towels stops it from moving under the cabinets and the floor. Do not vacuum unless the machine is rated for wet and dry use.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. Opening the door mid-cycle can release the whole load of water onto the floor.
  5. Photograph the Failed PartTake pictures of the hose, valve or fitting that failed and keep the part. It may be requested by the manufacturer of the appliance and by your insurance company.
Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • Refrigerators that leak at floor level because the drain pan has cracked or the defrost drain is clogged.
  • Washer supply hoses made of rubber that get weak over the years and burst from the pressure.
  • Plastic or copper ice maker lines that kink, crack or loosen at the fitting.
  • Standpipes that clog, or the drain hose from a washer coming loose from one.
  • Worn dishwasher door gaskets, cracked drain hoses or a failed pump seal.
What Is at Risk

What Happens if You Wait

Ceiling Below an Upstairs Laundry

The ceiling under an upper floor laundry catches whatever water makes it past the floor above. Drywall there sags and stains while the insulation over it holds water.

Rooms Next Door

A washer hose can release water for hours. Hallways, bedrooms and closets get wet as it travels under the walls, and carpet and pad are soaked a good distance from the laundry.

Hidden Mold at the Back

The warm, dark space behind a refrigerator or dishwasher rarely gets checked. Mold on the floor and wall can be fed by a slow drip in that spot well before it is noticed.

Cabinet Damage

From below, a leaking dishwasher wets the toe kicks and the side panels of the cabinets. The panels swell and separate, and wet cabinet boxes can hold moisture against the wall for weeks.

Coverage

What About Insurance?

A hose that bursts or a machine that overflows without warning is the kind of sudden and accidental event homeowners policies often cover. What is typically paid is the water damage, while the failed part and the appliance are left out. Slow seepage from a dishwasher or ice maker line is a harder claim, because gradual leaks usually are not covered. Certain insurers will want to know the hose's age and whether it was kept up. Hold on to the part that failed, photograph everything ahead of cleanup, and review what your policy says.

Pricing

What Affects the Cost

  • How long the leak ran before it was found, especially with a slow drip behind an appliance.
  • Presence of mold, which adds removal work in front of the drying.
  • Whether cabinets and flooring can be dried in place or need to be removed.
  • Which appliance leaked and whether it was a fast flood or a slow seep.
